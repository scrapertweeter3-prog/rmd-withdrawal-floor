The IRS withdrawal floor crosses 4 percent at 75

A traditional retirement account, an IRA or a 401(k) left behind at an old employer, answers to two withdrawal schedules. The plan says 4 percent of the starting balance, adjusted up for inflation each year, for as long as the money lasts. The IRS runs its own schedule, and its schedule is a floor. The RMD age depends on the birth year: 73 for anyone born 1951 through 1959, 75 for anyone born in 1960 or later. From that age on, a share of the prior December 31 balance has to come out every year, and a miss costs a 25 percent excise on the shortfall, cut to 10 percent if the withdrawal is made up inside the correction window. The plan may always take more than the floor. It may never take less.

The share comes from the uniform lifetime table, which covers most retirees. It starts at 26.5 at 73, so 1,000,000 / 26.5 = $37,736, a 3.77 percent floor, and it tightens every year after: 25.5 at 74 for $39,216, 3.92 percent; 24.6 at 75 for $40,650, 4.07 percent; 23.7 at 76, 4.22 percent; 20.2 at 80 for $49,505, 4.95 percent; 16.0 at 85 for $62,500, 6.25 percent. Set against the plan's 4 percent on the same million, the floor is slack at 73 and 74 and crosses the line between 74 and 75. From 75 on, the table forces more out of the account than the plan puts in the budget.

The forced excess is the price of the crossover. Per 1,000,000 of balance, the floor demands 40,650 at 75, 650 over the plan's 40,000; 49,505 at 80, 9,505 over; 62,500 at 85, 22,500 over. Every forced dollar is ordinary income in the year it lands, stacked on top of whatever else that year already earns, whether or not the spending plan has a use for it. The 4 percent debate is an argument about how much the portfolio can spare. After 75 a printed table settles the question, and the portfolio does not get a vote.

The slack years are the ones the owner still controls. At 73 and 74 the floor sits 2,264 and 784 under the plan's 40,000 per million, and before 73 the table is empty altogether. The stretch between the last paycheck and the first forced withdrawal is the only window where both the amount and the tax bracket get chosen instead of assigned. Moving 50,000 a year out of the traditional account and into a Roth during a 22 percent year costs 11,000 in tax, paid once, on the owner's calendar. The same 50,000 forced out at 80 under a 24 percent bracket costs 12,000, and each year after that the table forces out another 50,000 and taxes it at whatever bracket the stack has reached.

The plan side of the math gets all the argument: survival checks, guardrails, the calendar of the draw. The floor side is a printed table, and from 75 it is the binding one. Both halves are the withdrawal story at Firenomics, where the guides live at firenomics.com.

The plan draws 4 percent. The table draws what it must.
